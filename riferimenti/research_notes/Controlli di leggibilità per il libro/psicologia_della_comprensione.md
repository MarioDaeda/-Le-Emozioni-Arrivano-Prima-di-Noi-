## 1. Cohesion and coherence (Kintsch construction-integration, situation model, Coh-Metrix, reverse cohesion effect, given-new, topic continuity, pronouns, causal connectives)

### Takeaway
Readers build three levels of representation: surface code, textbase and situation model. Explicit cohesion cues (repeated arguments, explicit causal connectives, clear referents, given-before-new ordering) reliably help low-knowledge readers build the textbase and recall the text. For readers with high knowledge the benefit can shrink or even reverse (the "reverse cohesion effect"), but that effect is fragile and depends on the reader being strategic. SME managers are knowledgeable about business and novices in neuroscience and psychology. So the manuscript should use high cohesion in the scientific explanations. In the narrative and business-practice passages, cohesion can be lighter.

### Cited Findings
- **Construction-integration model.** Comprehension happens in two phases: a loose, bottom-up *construction* of propositions and associations, then a constraint-satisfaction *integration* that keeps the propositions that fit together. The output is a textbase plus a situation model that merges the text with prior knowledge — [Kintsch 1988, Psychological Review 95(2):163–182 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.95.2.163); earlier processing-cycle model with a limited buffer of propositions carried over between cycles — [Kintsch & van Dijk 1978, Psychological Review 85(5):363 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.85.5.363). Evidence strength: the framework is STRONG, widely used and the basis of Coh-Metrix.
- **Situation-model dimensions.** Readers keep track of five dimensions: time, space, causation, intentionality (goals) and protagonist. A break in any of them (a time jump, a new location, a new character, a broken causal chain) raises reading time and weakens connections in memory. This is the event-indexing model — [Zwaan & Radvansky 1998, Psychological Bulletin 123(2):162 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.123.2.162). STRONG.
- **Causal-chain centrality predicts recall in narratives.** Story statements with more causal connections, and those on the main causal chain, are recalled better and rated more important — [Trabasso & van den Broek 1985, Journal of Memory and Language 24:612–630 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(85)90049-X). STRONG and much replicated. Readers routinely generate causal and goal inferences while reading — [Graesser, Singer & Trabasso 1994, Psychological Review 101(3):371 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.3.371).
- **Explicit connectives.** Explicitly marking a coherence relation (for example with "because" or "so") speeds the processing of the following sentence. Causal relations are processed faster and recalled better than additive ones — [Sanders & Noordman 2000, Discourse Processes 29(1):37–60 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326950dp2901_3). MODERATE.
- **Given-new contract.** Readers look for the antecedent of "given" information in memory and then attach the "new" information to it. When no direct antecedent exists, readers must make a *bridging inference*, which measurably slows reading. Classic example: "We got some beer out of the car. The beer was warm" (direct) is faster than "We checked the picnic supplies. The beer was warm" (bridging) — [Haviland & Clark 1974, Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior 13:512–521 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4). STRONG, a classic result.
- **Pronouns and repeated names.** When the referent is the salient topic (the discourse center), repeating the full name instead of using a pronoun *slows* reading. This is the "repeated-name penalty" — [Gordon, Grosz & Gilliom 1993, Cognitive Science 17(3):311–347 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1703_1). Implication: pronouns are good for a continuing topic. A name is better after a topic shift or when two same-gender characters are both active.
- **Italian-specific pronoun resolution.** In Italian a *null* subject (pro-drop) is preferentially read as referring to the antecedent in subject position. An *overt* pronoun (lui/lei) tends to refer to a non-subject antecedent. This is the Position of Antecedent Strategy — [Carminati 2002, PhD dissertation, UMass Amherst †](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/dissertations/AAI3056226). MODERATE (replicated in several Italian experiments, but the evidence is psycholinguistic and at sentence level).
- **Coh-Metrix indices.** The tool computes the following measures: referential cohesion (noun, argument, stem and content-word overlap between adjacent sentences and across all sentences); LSA semantic overlap; connective incidence (causal, logical, adversative, temporal, additive); causal cohesion (the ratio of causal particles and connectives to causal verbs); situation-model indices; and syntax and word measures — [Graesser, McNamara, Louwerse & Cai 2004, Behavior Research Methods, Instruments & Computers 36:193–202 †](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03195564). There are five "Text Easability" principal components: narrativity, syntactic simplicity, word concreteness, referential cohesion and deep (causal) cohesion — [Graesser, McNamara & Kulikowich 2011, Educational Researcher 40(5):223–234 †](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X11413260). The ASU SoLET lab keeps a publication list on cohesion — [SoLET cohesion publications [V]](https://soletlab.asu.edu/cohesion-publications/).
- **Reverse cohesion effect.** In McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996, *Cognition and Instruction* 14(1):1–43, doi 10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1), low-knowledge readers benefited from high-cohesion texts. High-knowledge readers did better on *situation-model* measures (inference, problem solving) with low-cohesion texts, presumably because the gaps forced them to generate inferences — [summary in Frontiers in Education 2020 [V]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.00026/epub); [BYU Editing Research summary [V]](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing). In a companion paper, high-coherence text had an advantage on recall and multiple-choice measures — [McNamara & Kintsch 1996, Discourse Processes 22(3):247–288 [V]](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/2804815).
- **Fragility of the reverse effect.** One review notes the pattern "has not been unambiguously supported", and that coherence sometimes had no effect on situation-model measures — [Long et al. 2006, UW CCDL [V]](https://sites.uw.edu/ccdl/files//2013/12/Long_Assessing-text_2006.pdf). O'Reilly & McNamara found that high-cohesion texts can be *better* for high-knowledge readers who are also strategic — [O'Reilly & McNamara 2007, Discourse Processes 43(2):121–152 [V: citation only]](https://soletlab.asu.edu/cohesion-publications/). A 2020 study of 511 high-school students found strong prior-knowledge effects and no overall cohesion effect (preprint, not peer reviewed) — [McCarthy et al. 2020, EasyChair preprint [V]](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/4Krz). Prior knowledge usually explains more variance than cohesion manipulations — [Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara 2009, Learning and Instruction 19(3):228–242 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.04.003). Evidence on the reverse effect: WEAK to MODERATE. The basic benefit of cohesion for low-knowledge readers: MODERATE to STRONG.
- **Expertise reversal (a parallel effect from cognitive load theory).** Instructional support that helps novices becomes redundant, and can even hurt, as expertise grows — [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, Educational Psychologist 38(1):23–31 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**C1. Given-new check (per sentence pair).** Rule: the first noun phrase or topic of sentence *n+1* should refer to something already introduced in sentences *n−2..n* (repeated, a synonym, a pronoun, or an obvious part or whole). Flag sentences that open with a new technical noun phrase that has no anchor. HEURISTIC threshold: flag a paragraph if more than 1 in 3 sentences starts with a new, unanchored topic.
- FAIL: "Marco chiuse la telefonata con il fornitore. L'amigdala attiva la risposta di allarme in circa un quinto di secondo." (The amygdala appears from nowhere, so the reader has to bridge alone.)
- PASS: "Marco chiuse la telefonata con il fornitore, il cuore ancora in gola. Quel tuffo al cuore ha un'origine precisa: l'amigdala, una piccola struttura del cervello che fa scattare l'allarme in circa un quinto di secondo."

**C2. Causal-connective check (science passages).** Every claim of the form "X causes/leads to/explains Y" should be marked with an explicit connective: perché, quindi, di conseguenza, per questo, così, il che significa che. Flag adjacent sentences where a causal relation is implied but left unmarked. The same applies to contrast (ma, però, invece, eppure). HEURISTIC: in an explanatory paragraph, at least one explicit causal or adversative connective per 2–3 sentences. Do *not* fire the check on narrative scenes, where chronology already supplies the coherence.
- FAIL: "Lo stress cronico alza il cortisolo. La memoria di lavoro peggiora. Le riunioni del lunedì diventano un campo minato."
- PASS: "Lo stress cronico tiene alto il cortisolo, e **questo** riduce la memoria di lavoro; **per questo** le riunioni del lunedì, piene di decisioni rapide, diventano un campo minato."

**C3. Situation-model continuity check (narrative).** At every scene break, check that time, place and protagonist are re-established within the first 1–2 sentences ("Il martedì successivo, in magazzino, Giulia…"). Flag shifts in time, space or protagonist that are signalled only implicitly. Grounded in event indexing (Zwaan & Radvansky).

**C4. Pronoun and reference check (Italian).** (a) If two characters of the same gender are both active in the last 2 sentences, flag any null subject or "lui/lei" whose referent is ambiguous. (b) A null subject should refer to the previous *subject*. Flag cases where a null subject is meant to refer to the previous object (Carminati). (c) After a topic shift or a gap of one paragraph or more, re-introduce the full name. (d) Do NOT replace pronouns with names when the referent is the continuing topic (repeated-name penalty).
- FAIL: "Paolo ha detto a Luca che era stato licenziato." (Who was fired?)
- PASS: "Paolo ha detto a Luca: 'Mi hanno licenziato'."

**C5. Causal-chain centrality.** In each chapter, the scientific point should sit *on* the character's causal chain: the emotion explains a decision, which has a consequence. It should not be a side branch. Flag explanation blocks that do not connect causally back to the story event before or after. (Trabasso & van den Broek: causally central content is better recalled.)

**C6. Cohesion level by audience segment.** Apply the strict C1–C2 checks to neuroscience and psychology content, where readers are low-knowledge. Apply them more loosely to business-practice content, where SME managers are high-knowledge and the expertise-reversal and reverse cohesion effects make over-explaining a risk for annoyance and redundancy. The evidence that "low cohesion is better for experts" is not strong enough to justify deliberately removing cohesion.

**Automatable proxies:** argument overlap between adjacent sentences (shared lemmatized nouns or pronouns referring to them); connective counts by category per 1,000 words; flagging paragraphs with zero overlap between adjacent sentences.

### Gaps
- No validated Italian Coh-Metrix was found in this session. Italian readability tools exist (for example READ-IT by Dell'Orletta, Montemagni & Venturi 2011, ILC-CNR †), but their cohesion indices were not verified here.
- No empirical threshold exists for "the right amount" of connectives or argument overlap. Coh-Metrix gives normed distributions by grade level for English, not cut-offs.
- The original 1996 papers were not opened; the effects come from secondary summaries.

## 2. Concreteness and imageability (dual coding, Sadoski & Paivio, concreteness effect, norms, concreteness fading)

### Takeaway
Concreteness is one of the strongest single text-level predictors of how comprehensible, interesting and memorable readers find a text, often stronger than readability formulas. Abstract psychological concepts (regulation, appraisal, interoception) should be anchored to a concrete, imageable instance: a scene, an object, a bodily sensation. The idea of "concreteness fading" (start concrete, then explicitly move to the abstract principle) comes from maths and science instruction and has decent support there.

### Cited Findings
- **Dual coding theory.** Concrete language activates both a verbal code and an imagery code. Abstract language mostly activates only the verbal code. The two codes are additive for memory — [Paivio 1991, Canadian Journal of Psychology 45(3):255–287 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0084295); book-length application to reading in *Imagery and Text* — [Sadoski & Paivio 2013, 2nd ed., Routledge †](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203801932). The word-level concreteness effect on recall is STRONG. Dual coding as the *explanation* is debated: context-availability theory is the main rival.
- **Concreteness in real texts.** Concreteness was the variable most strongly related to comprehensibility and recall of text passages — [Sadoski, Goetz & Fritz 1993, JEP 85(2):291–304, as described in PeerJ preprint [V]](https://peerj.com/preprints/2719.pdf). Across four text types, concrete texts were recalled better than abstract ones and rated more comprehensible and interesting. The size of the recall advantage varied by text type — [Sadoski, Goetz & Rodriguez 2000, JEP 92(1):85–95, per secondary summaries [V]](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/442/44249256010.pdf); [popular summary, Wylie Communications [V]](https://www.wyliecomm.com/2020/02/concrete-images-in-writing). MODERATE to STRONG (several studies from one lab, consistent direction).
- **Concreteness fading.** A systematic review recommends starting with concrete materials and then "explicitly and gradually fading to the more abstract", typically in three steps: enactive or physical, then iconic, then symbolic — [Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone 2014, Educational Psychology Review 26:9–25 [V]](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-014-9249-3); [Indiana PCL summary [V]](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=885). A follow-up argues the theory needs clearer definitions — [Fyfe & Nathan 2019, Educational Review [V]](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/f1ea5994-0d9f-4215-abec-152a03149277). Evidence is MODERATE and comes mostly from maths and science learning with children and students, not adult prose reading.
- **English norms.** Concreteness ratings for about 40,000 English lemmas on a 1–5 scale — [Brysbaert, Warriner & Kuperman 2014, Behavior Research Methods 46:904–911 †](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-013-0403-5).
- **Italian norms (usable for automated checks):**
  - 417 Italian words rated by 250 participants on age of acquisition, concreteness, familiarity, context availability, imageability, abstractness and mode of acquisition — [Della Rosa, Catricalà, Vigliocco & Cappa 2010, Behavior Research Methods 42(4):1042–1048 [V]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BRM.42.4.1042); [NORARE dataset listing [V]](https://norare.clld.org/contributions/DellaRosa-2010-417).
  - The Italian adaptation of ANEW: 1,121 words with valence, arousal and dominance plus familiarity, imageability and concreteness — [Montefinese, Ambrosini, Fairfield & Mammarella 2014, Behavior Research Methods 46:887–903, doi 10.3758/s13428-013-0405-3 [V: abstract-level]](https://ricerca.unich.it/handle/11564/641643). This is especially useful because it also has emotional ratings, which fits a book on emotions.
  - Age-of-acquisition norms by Montefinese et al. 2019, with concreteness and imageability covariates; data on OSF (osf.io/3trg2) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019 [V]](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00278/text).
  - Italian perceptual strength (Lancaster-style sensorimotor) norms — [Vergallito, Petilli & Marelli 2020, Behavior Research Methods †; draft PDF [V]](https://boa.unimib.it/retrieve/e39773b6-69c2-35a3-e053-3a05fe0aac26/Draft_Perceptual_Strength_Norms_Italian_Final.pdf).
  - Imageability and other norms for 626 Italian nouns — [Barca, Burani & Arduino 2002, BRMIC 34(3):424–434 †](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03195471).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**K1. Anchor rule (concreteness fading for prose).** Every abstract technical concept (for example "regolazione emotiva", "valutazione cognitiva/appraisal", "interocezione", "carico allostatico") must have a concrete, imageable instance *within the same or the adjacent paragraph*. The instance can be a perceivable action, object, body sensation, number or scene. The order should preferably be concrete, then abstract label, then generalization. Flag concepts introduced only through other abstract words. HEURISTIC.
- FAIL: "La regolazione emotiva consiste nella modulazione dei processi di valutazione che determinano l'intensità e la durata della risposta affettiva."
- PASS: "Quando Sara sente salire la rabbia davanti al cliente e, prima di rispondere, si chiede 'che cosa vuole davvero?', sta facendo ciò che gli psicologi chiamano *rivalutazione*: cambiare il significato della situazione per cambiare l'emozione."

**K2. Abstract-noun density.** Count abstract nouns (Italian norms: concreteness rating below scale midpoint; morphological proxies: -zione, -mento, -ità, -ismo, -enza/-anza). HEURISTIC flag: a sentence with 3 or more abstract nouns and no concrete noun; a paragraph where more than about 60% of content nouns are abstract. Validate against the Della Rosa and Montefinese norms where the words are covered.

**K3. Return to the scene.** After an explanatory block, the text should return to the concrete character scene so the reader can re-apply the abstraction, closing the concrete–abstract–concrete loop. Flag explanation blocks longer than about 300–400 words without any concrete referent (HEURISTIC).

**K4. Bodily and emotional vocabulary is an asset.** Emotion words carry imageability and arousal, so emotional, concrete vocabulary tends to be memorable. The Montefinese 2014 norms can rank candidate words.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures concreteness fading specifically in adult narrative nonfiction. The transfer from maths instruction is an inference.
- The full texts of Sadoski 2000 and the effect sizes were not retrieved, and sample details in the popular summaries are unverified.
- Coverage of the Italian norms is limited (417 to about 1,100 words), so automated checking will need morphological proxies or a model-based extrapolation of concreteness.

## 3. Cognitive load theory, working-memory limits, signaling and coherence principles

### Takeaway
Working memory holds roughly 3–5 chunks. New, interacting elements (high element interactivity) load it, and familiar schemas compress it. For a lay reader this argues for introducing few new technical concepts at a time, building each one before combining them, and removing interesting but irrelevant material. Signaling (headings, previews, explicit "the key point is…") has a reliable, moderate positive effect on retention. These numbers come from lab and multimedia studies, so translating them into a figure like "concepts per paragraph" is a heuristic.

### Cited Findings
- **Capacity limit.** Cowan argues for a central capacity limit averaging about 4 chunks (range 3–5) in young adults, and fewer in children and older adults — [Cowan 2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1):87–114, doi 10.1017/s0140525x01003922 [V]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/); [full PDF [V]](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf). STRONG as an order of magnitude. The exact number is debated in the BBS commentaries.
- **Cognitive load theory.** The theory distinguishes intrinsic load (set by element interactivity, meaning how many elements must be processed simultaneously) from extraneous load (caused by poor presentation). Germane load has been reconceptualized in later versions — [Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019, Educational Psychology Review 31:261–292 †](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5); original [Sweller 1988, Cognitive Science 12(2):257–285 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4). Critiques cover measurement problems (self-reported load) and the unfalsifiability of the load types — [de Jong 2010, Instructional Science 38:105–134 †](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0). The framework is MODERATE: well supported for instructional design, and its constructs are contested.
- **Signaling principle.** In a meta-analysis, signaling had a strong effect on retention and a moderate effect on transfer. Results for prior knowledge and dynamic signals were inconsistent across meta-analyses — [Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey 2018, Educational Research Review 23:1–24, via QIC-WD umbrella summary [V]](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Signaling%20in%20Training%20111021.pdf). Another meta-analysis found signaling increased learning outcomes, d = 0.38 — [Alpizar et al. 2020, ETR&D, WSU record [V]](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842). Text-specific signals (headings, previews, overviews, typographic cues) improve memory for topic structure — [Lorch 1989, Educational Psychology Review 1:209–234 †](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320135). MODERATE to STRONG.
- **Coherence principle.** People learn better when extraneous words, pictures and sounds are excluded. Mayer reports this principle as among the most consistently supported, with large median effect sizes (Mayer & Fiorella 2014 report roughly d ≈ 0.86 across about 23 tests — † to verify) — [Mayer (ed.), Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 2nd ed. 2014 †](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139547369). See also the seductive-details meta-analyses in section 8.
- **Expertise reversal** (see section 1): instructions that help novices can hurt experts — [Kalyuga et al. 2003 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**L1. New-concept budget.** Count *new* technical terms or concepts, meaning those not yet introduced in the book, per paragraph and per section. HEURISTIC thresholds derived from Cowan (about 4 chunks, with an interacting concept costing more than one chunk):
- ≤ 2 new interacting concepts per paragraph; ≤ 1 if the paragraph also asks the reader to relate them (for example "cortisol reduces prefrontal control, which increases amygdala reactivity").
- ≤ 4–5 new core concepts per chapter section (for example between two headings), each with a concrete anchor (K1) before the next one is introduced.
- FAIL: one paragraph introduces amigdala, corteccia prefrontale, cortisolo, asse HPA and memoria di lavoro together.
- PASS: amigdala in one paragraph with a scene, prefrontal cortex in the next, then their interaction in a third.

**L2. Build before combining (element interactivity).** When a later sentence requires holding concepts A, B and C together, check that A, B and C were each introduced and anchored earlier, ideally in separate sentences. Flag sentences that both define a concept and use it in a relation with another new concept.

**L3. Signaling checks.** (a) Each chapter or section opens with a 1–3 sentence preview of what the reader will understand (see also section 10 on advance organizers). (b) The key take-home claim of each scientific explanation is marked explicitly ("Il punto è questo:", "In sintesi:"). (c) Headings are informative, stating the claim rather than only a topic: "Perché la paura rende miopi le decisioni" rather than "La paura". (d) Do not over-signal: use bold or italics sparingly, for at most about one key term per paragraph (HEURISTIC).

**L4. Coherence (cut what doesn't serve).** For each sentence in an explanatory passage, ask whether it serves the target concept or the character's arc. Flag trivia, digressions about the history of a scientist or study, and number-dense asides that serve neither (link to section 8).

### Gaps
- No empirical study gives a "concepts per paragraph" limit for prose. The L1 numbers are an extrapolation from working-memory research and multimedia studies.
- The exact effect sizes in Schneider et al. 2018 were not retrieved, only the qualitative summary. The coherence-principle effect size is †.

## 4. Negation processing cost

### Takeaway
Negated sentences take longer to verify and are more error-prone than affirmatives. This is STRONG classic evidence, though the size of the cost depends on context. Experiential-simulation accounts suggest readers first represent the negated state ("the door is not open" brings up an open door) and only later the actual state. The cost largely disappears when the negation is *pragmatically licensed*, meaning it denies something the reader actually expected. Multiple negations compound the difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- **Verification cost.** In sentence-picture verification, negatives are verified more slowly than affirmatives, and "true negatives" are often slowest — [Clark & Chase 1972, Cognitive Psychology 3(3):472–517 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(72)90019-9). STRONG (classic and replicated).
- **Two-step simulation account.** After affirmative sentences, readers showed a match effect for the actual state at a 750 ms delay but not after negatives. At 1500 ms the effect appeared for negatives — [Kaup, Lüdtke & Zwaan 2006, Journal of Pragmatics 38(7):1033–1050 [V: abstract]](https://uni-tuebingen.de/securedl/sdl-eyJ0eXAiOiJKV1QiLCJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiJ9.eyJpYXQiOjE3MTk4OTgzNjgsImV4cCI6MTcxOTk4ODM2NywidXNlciI6MCwiZ3JvdXBzIjpbMCwtMV0sImZpbGUiOiJmaWxlYWRtaW5cL1VuaV9UdWViaW5nZW5cL0Zha3VsdGFldGVuXC9JbmZvS29nbmlcL1BJXC9PcmdhbmlzYXRpb25fQWJ0ZWlsdW5nZW5cL0FsbF9Qc3ljaG9fUHN5Y2hvTWV0aG9kXC9Lb2dTcHJhY2hlXC9LYXVwX2V0YWxfMDYucGRmIiwicGFnZSI6MTY1MTZ9.wwNuj0EaDgi1Zb9AFnaYRg8NiM8CCnd8uA7tDgozn_8/Kaup_etal_06.pdf). The delayed-integration account is contested. Pragmatic researchers argue for immediate integration, and Dudschig & Kaup note the two steps are "not mandatory" — [review in J Psycholinguist Res 2021 [V]](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10936-021-09797-w); [PMC review [V]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8660733). MODERATE, with the theory debated.
- **Pragmatic licensing removes much of the cost.** When a negation is pragmatically licensed, there is no N400 processing penalty, compared with unlicensed negations — [Nieuwland & Kuperberg 2008, Psychological Science 19(12):1213–1218 †](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02226.x). Negatives are easier when they deny a plausible expectation (the "plausible denial" context) — [Wason 1965, JVLVB 4:7–11 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(65)80060-3). MODERATE.
- **Multiple negations.** Each added negative element raises comprehension time and errors — [Sherman 1976, JVLVB 15(2):143–157 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5371(76)90015-3). WEAK to MODERATE (an old study, but consistent with everything else).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**N1. Flag unlicensed negation in explanations.** A negation is acceptable when it corrects a belief the reader plausibly holds, for example "Le emozioni **non** sono il contrario della razionalità" as a deliberate myth-buster. Flag negations that state facts the reader had no reason to expect otherwise, and rewrite them in affirmative form.
- FAIL: "Il cortisolo non resta basso quando lo stress non si interrompe."
- PASS: "Se lo stress continua, il cortisolo resta alto."

**N2. Double and multiple negation.** Flag any clause with 2 or more negative elements: non, nessuno, niente, mai, senza, né, negative-prefix adjectives (in-/im-/dis-), negative verbs (negare, evitare, impedire, mancare). EMPIRICAL direction (Sherman), HEURISTIC cut-off at 2.
- FAIL: "Non è infrequente che un manager non riesca a non reagire."
- PASS: "Capita spesso che un manager reagisca d'istinto, anche quando vorrebbe trattenersi."

**N3. State the positive after a myth-busting negation.** After "X non è Y", the next clause should give the correct positive model ("…è Z"). Otherwise the reader is left with only the negated (wrong) representation active, as the two-step account predicts.

**N4. Instructions and advice.** Phrase recommendations to managers in positive form ("fai una pausa di dieci secondi") rather than negative form ("non reagire subito"), unless the contrast is the point.

### Gaps
- No Italian-specific experiments on negation cost in expository prose were found. The constructions with "non… mica" and "non… affatto", and negative concord in Italian ("non ho visto nessuno"), may not carry the same cost as double negation in English. This is an important caution: Italian negative concord ("non… nessuno/niente/mai") is ONE semantic negation, and a check should not count it as a double negative.
- The effect sizes from Clark & Chase and Sherman were not retrieved.

## 5. Passive voice and nominalizations

### Takeaway
The evidence on passives is mixed. Reversible and implausible passives are misinterpreted more often than actives in "good-enough" processing studies, but that finding depends on the task. Passives are *not* uniformly slower to read, and they can serve the given-new and topic-continuity principle. Nominalizations are read longer in eye-tracking studies, and they hide the agent and the action, which matters for a narrative about emotions and decisions. Evidence on nominalizations is limited and older. The strongest practical case against them comes from concreteness (section 2) and agent visibility rather than from large direct experiments.

### Cited Findings
- **Good-enough misinterpretation.** Participants misinterpreted implausible passives ("The dog was bitten by the man") more often than actives, assigning thematic roles by plausibility or heuristics such as agent-before-patient — [Ferreira 2003, Cognitive Psychology 47(2):164–203, summaries [V]](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/preview/5565210/OnlineRepresentationsOfNonCanonicalSentencesACCEPTED.pdf). The accuracy data have been replicated — [CUNY 2019 abstract, Burnsky & Staub [V]](https://www.colorado.edu/event/cuny2019/sites/default/files/attached-files/c16_burnsky_staub.pdf).
- **Challenge from task demands.** Bader & Meng argue the errors stem from the difficulty of retrieving information when a question probes a sentence held in memory. With more global comprehension tasks (yes/no questions, plausibility judgments) there are few or no errors — [summarized in Meng & Bader 2021 / CUNY 2021 abstracts [V]](https://www.cuny2021.io/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/CUNY_2021_abstract_136.pdf). A 2019 study found passives were consistently read *faster* than actives online, but were more error-prone when the question targeted who did what to whom ("Passives are not hard to interpret but hard to remember") — [Taylor & Francis figshare [V]](https://tandf.figshare.com/articles/Passives_are_not_hard_to_interpret_but_hard_to_remember_evidence_from_online_and_offline_studies/7962557/1). MODERATE evidence that passives carry a memory and role-assignment cost, mainly for reversible or implausible events.
- **Reversibility matters.** Early difficulties with passives were restricted to reversible passives — [Slobin 1966 per Balling (CBS) [V]](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/files/57294517/laura_winther_balling_no_effect_acceptedmanuscript.pdf). An eye-tracking study of authentic texts found no difference between "recommended" and "problem" constructions — [Balling, accepted manuscript [V]](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/files/57294517/laura_winther_balling_no_effect_acceptedmanuscript.pdf).
- **Nominalizations.** In eye-tracking of German legal texts, nominalizations were read significantly longer than other nouns, and reformulated texts were read faster overall — [Wolfer, per search summary; Fachsprache journal [V]](https://ejournals.facultas.at/index.php/fachsprache/article/download/1340/1219). Recall was better for subject nominalizations with simpler underlying structure (N = 20, an old small study) — [Rohrman & Polzella 1968, Psychonomic Science 12:376 [V]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03331360). A legal-writing review claims passives and nominalizations make text slower, harder to understand and remember, and less engaging. This is advocacy literature, not a meta-analysis — [Carpenter 2022, ALWD [V]](https://alwd.org/lcr-archives/fall-2022-volume-19/660-the-problems-and-positives-of-passives). WEAK to MODERATE overall.
- The Coleman study requested in the brief (Coleman 1964, "The comprehensibility of several grammatical transformations", J. Applied Psychology †) could not be verified in this session.

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**P1. Agent visibility (stronger rationale for this book).** In narrative and decision passages, the emotional agent (a character, "il cervello", "l'amigdala") should be the grammatical subject of an active verb. Flag agentless passives and impersonal "si" constructions where the agent matters to the story or to the causal explanation.
- FAIL: "Fu presa la decisione di rinviare il lancio, a causa della percezione di un rischio eccessivo."
- PASS: "Giulia decise di rinviare il lancio: sentiva che il rischio era troppo alto."

**P2. Reversible passives.** Flag passives where both participants could plausibly be the agent ("Il direttore fu criticato dal socio", "Luca fu interrotto da Marta"). These are the cases with documented misinterpretation. Rewrite them as actives.

**P3. Allowed passives.** Do NOT flag passives that keep the given topic in subject position (the given-new principle from section 1), as in "Marco aveva sottovalutato la riunione. La riunione era stata convocata dal nuovo socio…". Do not flag scientific passives where the agent is unknown or irrelevant ("Il cortisolo viene rilasciato…").

**P4. Nominalization density.** Flag clauses where an action is expressed as a noun (-zione, -mento, -enza, -tura) and paired with a light verb ("effettuare una valutazione", "procedere alla realizzazione", "avere una reazione"). HEURISTIC: flag a sentence with 2 or more nominalized actions, or a nominalization chain ("la gestione della percezione della valutazione"). Italian bureaucratic style ("stile nominale") makes this especially relevant.
- FAIL: "L'attivazione della risposta di allarme comporta una riduzione della capacità di valutazione."
- PASS: "Quando scatta l'allarme, valutiamo peggio."

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis on passives or nominalizations in expository prose comprehension was found.
- The Coleman 1964 and Spyridakis & Isakson (1998, nominalizations and recall) findings could not be verified.
- No Italian-specific experimental evidence was found on the "si" passive or impersonal constructions.

## 6. Syntactic complexity: dependency length, center-embedding, garden paths, heavy pre-verbal constituents (Italian incisi)

### Takeaway
Processing cost grows with the distance (in intervening new discourse referents) between words that must be linked, such as subject and verb, or verb and object. Languages, including Italian, tend to minimize dependency lengths in production. For Italian prose this means long incisi and heavy left-branching material placed between subject and main verb, or before the main clause, are the main syntactic risk. Garden-path structures (a temporary misparse) are a separate, well-established source of difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- **Locality theory.** Gibson's theory has an integration cost (linking an incoming word to its head, which grows with distance) and a memory cost (tracking incomplete syntactic requirements) — [Gibson 1998, Cognition 68:1–76 [V: abstract]](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf). In the Dependency Locality Theory (Gibson 2000), distance is measured by the number of *new discourse referents* intervening between the endpoints — [Warren & Gibson 2002, Cognition [V]](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Warren_&_Gibson_2002_Cogn.pdf). In Gibson's summary, "many on-line processing effects follow from a cognitive cost to connecting materials that are farther apart" — [Gibson, Syntax, MIT Press 2025 [V]](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Syntax_Gibson_MITPress_2025.pdf). STRONG for the general locality effect, with debate about expectation-based accounts as complementary.
- **Dependency length minimization across languages.** Producers prefer shorter dependencies (shown for written English in the Penn Treebank) — [Temperley 2007, Cognition, per Gibson citations [V]](https://nats-www.informatik.uni-hamburg.de/bin/view/CDG/SentenceProcessingReading). Across 37 languages, including Italian, dependency lengths are shorter than random baselines — [Futrell, Mahowald & Gibson 2015, PNAS 112(33):10336 †](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1502134112). STRONG.
- **Center-embedding.** Object-extracted relative clauses and doubly center-embedded clauses are hard ("The reporter who the senator attacked admitted the error"). This is a central DLT prediction — [Gibson 1998 [V]](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf). STRONG.
- **Garden paths.** Temporarily ambiguous structures cause regressions and longer reading times when the initially preferred parse turns out to be wrong — [Frazier & Rayner 1982, Cognitive Psychology 14(2):178–210 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(82)90008-1). STRONG.

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**S1. Subject–verb distance (Italian incisi).** Measure the number of words, and especially the new referents, between the subject and its main finite verb. HEURISTIC thresholds (derived from DLT, not empirically calibrated for Italian): flag if more than about 12 words or 3 or more new referents intervene; flag always if the material includes a full clause with its own verb.
- FAIL: "Il direttore commerciale, che da quando l'azienda, fondata dal padre nel 1987, aveva perso il suo cliente principale passava le notti a rivedere i numeri, decise di chiamare la banca."
- PASS: "Da quando l'azienda aveva perso il suo cliente principale, il direttore commerciale passava le notti a rivedere i numeri. Una mattina decise di chiamare la banca."

**S2. Heavy constituents before the main clause.** Flag sentences where more than about 20 words, or 2 or more subordinate or participial clauses ("Dopo aver…, considerato che…, pur sapendo che…"), come before the main verb. Move the heavy material after the main clause, or split the sentence. Italian allows postverbal subjects and flexible order, so prefer "heavy last".

**S3. Center-embedding and stacked relatives.** Flag any relative clause nested inside another ("che… che…") and any object relative whose subject is a full noun phrase ("il cliente che il fornitore aveva convinto"). Rewrite as two sentences.

**S4. Garden-path ambiguity.** Flag temporary ambiguities, especially in Italian with "che" (relative pronoun vs. complementizer), clitic attachment and preverbal objects with null subjects. Typical case: "Il manager ha detto al collaboratore che aveva sbagliato…" (who had made the mistake: a relative or a complement clause?).

**S5. Sentence length as a coarse proxy.** Not a causal variable in itself, but correlated with dependency length. HEURISTIC: flag explanatory sentences over about 30–35 words. Long narrative sentences with paratactic structure (coordinated, short dependencies) are acceptable.

### Gaps
- No Italian-calibrated thresholds for dependency length in reading were found. The figures are heuristics.
- Dependency length can be computed automatically with an Italian Universal Dependencies parser (for example Stanza or spaCy it_core), but that is a tooling recommendation, not an evidence claim.

## 7. Numbers and statistics in text (numeracy, natural frequencies, rounding, relative vs absolute risk)

### Takeaway
Many educated adults struggle with probabilities and percentages. Natural frequencies ("8 su 100") improve understanding over probabilities, though most people still fail complex Bayesian problems. Relative risk reductions are perceived as larger and more persuasive, but are not better understood, than absolute ones. Less numeric information, well chosen, improves comprehension especially for less numerate readers. Italian adults score below the OECD average in numeracy (†), which strengthens the case for sparse, rounded, frequency-format figures.

### Cited Findings
- **Natural frequencies vs probabilities.** In the original study, Bayesian accuracy was about 16% with probabilities vs about 46% with natural frequencies — [Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995, Psychological Review 102(4):684 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684). In McDowell & Jacobs' meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin 2017 †, doi 10.1037/bul0000126), natural frequencies raised solution rates to about 24% vs about 4% for probabilities; about three quarters still failed — [reported by Weber, Binder & Krauss 2018, Frontiers in Psychology [V]](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6194348/). The effect was smaller when both formats used visualizations, or when the formats were matched — [SMDM 2016 abstract [V]](https://smdm.confex.com/smdm/16BEC/webprogram/Paper9915.html). STRONG for direction, modest in absolute size.
- **Relative vs absolute risk (Cochrane review, 35 studies).** Natural frequencies were understood better than probabilities (moderate-quality evidence). RRR vs ARR: little or no difference in understanding, but RRR was perceived as larger and more persuasive. ARR was better understood than NNT — [Akl et al. 2011, Cochrane CD006776.pub2 [V]](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006776.pub2/related-content/podcast/18978); [ScienceDaily summary [V]](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/03/110316084421.htm). STRONG (systematic review).
- **Numeracy moderates effects.** Less numerate people are more influenced by framing and mood and draw less precise affective meaning from numbers — [Peters et al. 2006, Psychological Science 17(5):407–413 †](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01720.x). In "Less is more", reducing the amount of information presented, and making the important information easier to evaluate, improved comprehension and choice, especially for less numerate participants — [Peters, Dieckmann, Dixon, Hibbard & Mertz 2007, Medical Care Research and Review 64(2):169–190 †](https://doi.org/10.1177/10775587070640020301). Review: [Reyna, Nelson, Han & Dieckmann 2009, Psychological Bulletin 135(6):943 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017327). Fuzzy-trace theory says people rely on the *gist* of numbers (for example "small risk" vs "big risk"), so text should state the gist explicitly — [Reyna 2008, Medical Decision Making 28(6):850 †](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X08327066). MODERATE to STRONG.
- **Visual aids** (icon arrays) help especially low-numeracy readers — [Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2013, Current Directions in Psychological Science 22(5):392 †](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721413491570).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**Q1. Numbers per paragraph.** HEURISTIC (from "less is more" and working-memory limits, no direct empirical cut-off): ≤ 2 statistics per paragraph in narrative or explanatory prose; ≤ 1 if the reader has to compare them. More figures belong in a box, table or note.

**Q2. Rounding and precision.** Round to the precision that serves the gist: "circa un terzo" or "quasi il 40%" rather than "37,8%", unless the exact figure matters. Never show more significant digits than the study supports. HEURISTIC, grounded in fuzzy-trace theory.

**Q3. Frequency format.** Prefer "8 manager su 100" to "l'8%" or "una probabilità di 0,08" when reporting proportions or risks. Use a consistent denominator when comparing ("8 su 100 contro 2 su 100", not "8% contro 1 su 50").

**Q4. Relative claims need the absolute base.** Flag any "aumenta del X%", "raddoppia", "dimezza" or "X volte più probabile" that lacks the baseline. PASS: "Il rischio di errore sale da 4 a 6 decisioni su 100: un aumento del 50%, ma di sole 2 decisioni su 100."

**Q5. State the gist.** Each number should be followed or preceded by its meaning for the reader ("cioè: quasi uno su due"; "un effetto piccolo ma reale").

**Q6. Effect sizes in the book.** When the book reports a psychological effect, translate the effect size into words ("piccolo", "moderato") or into a frequency, rather than giving d or r values in the running text.

### Gaps
- No study was found that gives an empirical maximum number of figures per paragraph.
- The Italian numeracy statistic (PIAAC) was not verified this session, so it is marked †. The OECD "Survey of Adult Skills 2023: Italy" country note should be checked.
- The primary McDowell & Jacobs 2017 paper was not opened; the figures are via secondary sources.

## 8. Analogies, examples, worked examples, elaborative interrogation and the seductive details effect

### Takeaway
Analogies help when the *relational structure* maps clearly onto the target, and readers often fail to transfer from a single analogy unless the mapping is made explicit or two cases are compared. Comparing two cases to extract the common principle improved transfer for management students in negotiation training, a finding directly relevant to this book's audience. Interesting but irrelevant details ("seductive details") reliably hurt learning, with a small-to-moderate meta-analytic effect. Anecdotes must therefore carry the concept, not just decorate it.

### Cited Findings
- **Structure mapping.** Good analogies map *relations* (causes, goals) rather than surface attributes, and systematic, connected relations are preferred — [Gentner 1983, Cognitive Science 7(2):155–170 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3). STRONG (theory with extensive support).
- **Spontaneous transfer is poor.** People given a story analogue often fail to apply it to a target problem unless given a hint to use it — [Gick & Holyoak 1980, Cognitive Psychology 12(3):306–355 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(80)90013-4). Comparing two analogues promotes schema induction and transfer — [Gick & Holyoak 1983, Cognitive Psychology 15(1):1–38 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(83)90002-6). STRONG.
- **Case comparison in management training.** Students who compared two negotiation cases were much more likely to use the contingent-contract strategy later than students who read the cases separately — [Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003, Journal of Educational Psychology 95(2):393–408 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393). MODERATE to STRONG (several experiments).
- **Worked examples.** For novices, studying worked examples beats solving equivalent problems; the effect fades with expertise — [Sweller & Cooper 1985, Cognition and Instruction 2(1):59–89 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3); review [Atkinson, Derry, Renkl & Wortham 2000, Review of Educational Research 70(2):181–214 †](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070002181). STRONG in problem-solving domains. Its application to prose about emotional skills is by analogy (for example a step-by-step worked example of reappraisal).
- **Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation.** Both are rated "moderate utility" in the major review. Practice testing and distributed practice are rated "high utility". Summarization, highlighting, rereading and imagery for text are rated "low" — [Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1):4–58 †](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266). MODERATE.
- **Seductive details.** "Keep it Coherent": a meta-analysis of 68 experiments found a negative effect on learning, g ≈ −0.33 per a secondary summary, moderated by format and placement; most included studies used short sessions — [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, Educational Psychology Review 32:707–734, doi 10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4 [V]](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842); [WSU news release [V]](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [QIC-WD summary [V]](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Seductive%20Details%20in%20Training%20041821.pdf). Secondary summaries conflict on whether placement and format moderate the effect. An earlier meta-analysis reported roughly d ≈ 0.30 for retention and d ≈ 0.48 for transfer favoring the version without seductive details — [Rey 2012, Educational Research Review 7(3):216–237 †](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003). The original studies inserted interesting but irrelevant illustrations or paragraphs (for example about lightning), which reduced recall of the main ideas and transfer — [Harp & Mayer 1998, Journal of Educational Psychology 90(3):414–434 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414); [Garner, Gillingham & White 1989, Cognition and Instruction 6(1):41–57 †](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0601_2). STRONG for the existence of the effect, small to moderate in size, and mostly measured on short lessons and student samples. Popular summary — [Learning and the Brain blog [V]](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**A1. Seductive-detail test for anecdotes.** For each anecdote, story beat or "curiosità": (a) Does it instantiate the target concept, meaning the emotion, the mechanism or the consequence? (b) Would removing it make the concept harder to understand or apply? If both answers are no, flag it as a seductive detail: shorten it, move it to a note or box, or tie it explicitly to the concept. Character scenes in this book are *not* seductive details when the scene is the concrete instance of the mechanism (K1). They become seductive when side plot, colour or trivia compete for attention with the explanation.
- FAIL: a paragraph on how Phineas Gage's iron rod was later exhibited in a Harvard museum, in a chapter about decision-making.
- PASS: Gage's change in decision-making and social behaviour, linked to the manager's situation.

**A2. Explicit mapping for analogies.** Each analogy ("l'amigdala è un sistema d'allarme antincendio") must state the mapped relation explicitly ("…scatta prima di sapere se c'è davvero fuoco: meglio un falso allarme che un incendio ignorato") and ideally note where the analogy breaks. Flag analogies that map only surface features.

**A3. Two-case comparison.** For the book's key principles, check whether at least two different cases illustrate the same mechanism and whether the text explicitly says what they share ("Ciò che accomuna Marco e Giulia è…"). This is the Gentner–Loewenstein–Thompson finding, and it is directly applicable to managers.

**A4. Worked example for skills.** When the book teaches a technique (for example labelling the emotion, reappraisal, a pause), check that at least one step-by-step worked example exists, with the character's actual words or thoughts at each step.

**A5. Why-questions.** Each scientific explanation could be framed around a "why" question the reader would ask ("Perché ci arrabbiamo di più quando siamo stanchi?") and answered explicitly. This draws on elaborative interrogation (moderate utility) and doubles as signaling.

### Gaps
- The effect-size table of Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 was not retrieved, and the secondary summaries conflict on moderators. Rey 2012 numbers are †.
- No direct evidence was found on seductive details in *book-length* narrative nonfiction read voluntarily, where interest and engagement matter more than in lab lessons. The trade-off between engagement and learning is therefore an open question for this genre.

## 9. Jargon and the curse of knowledge

### Takeaway
Jargon reduces processing fluency, and through that reduces engagement and persuasion and increases resistance to the message. One study found the effect persisted *even when definitions were provided*; another (a thesis) found that *well-defined* jargon was processed about as easily as no jargon. Experts systematically overestimate what others know (the curse of knowledge). So a jargon check cannot rely on the author's intuition; it needs an external criterion, such as a basic-vocabulary list or a reader test.

### Cited Findings
- **Jargon and processing.** In an experiment with N = 650, jargon impaired the processing of scientific information, which led to greater motivated resistance to persuasion, higher risk perceptions and lower support for technology adoption — [Bullock, Colón Amill, Shulman & Dixon 2019, Public Understanding of Science 28(7):845–853 [V]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31354058/); [full-text PDF [V]](https://comm.osu.edu/sites/comm.osu.edu/files/PUS%202019-%20Bullock%20et%20al..pdf). MODERATE (one large experiment).
- **Definitions do not fully rescue jargon.** In a separate experiment (N = 650), jargon disrupted fluent processing "even when definitions for the jargon terms are provided". It lowered readers' identification with science, their interest and their perceived understanding — [Shulman, Dixon, Bullock & Colón Amill 2020, Journal of Language and Social Psychology, supplementary materials [V]](https://sage.figshare.com/articles/Supplementary_Materials_Supplemental_material_for_The_Effects_of_Jargon_on_Processing_Fluency_Self-Perceptions_and_Scientific_Engagement/11770794); [OSU news [V]](https://news.osu.edu/the-use-of-jargon-kills-peoples-interest-in-science-politics/).
- **Partly contradicting evidence.** A 2024 University of Alberta thesis found well-defined jargon did not reduce processing fluency, while poorly defined or undefined jargon did. So the *quality* of the definition matters — [U Alberta ERA thesis [V]](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/3a9183ad-babd-4721-8736-1e67c63765cc/download/68173a90-2547-4167-a9ca-6ca6ea2272c2). This is a thesis, so it is WEAK evidence. In a crisis context (COVID-19), jargon had no effect on persuasion, while it reduced persuasion for less urgent topics — [Shulman & Bullock et al. 2020, PLOS ONE (PMC) [V]](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7540871/).
- **Curse of knowledge.** Better-informed agents fail to ignore their own information when predicting what less-informed others will judge — [Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989, Journal of Political Economy 97(5):1232–1254 †](https://doi.org/10.1086/261651). Experts underestimate how long novices take to perform tasks — [Hinds 1999, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied 5(2):205–221 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.5.2.205). In the "tappers and listeners" study, tappers predicted listeners would identify about 50% of tapped songs, while listeners identified about 2.5% (Newton 1990, Stanford PhD dissertation †). The study was popularized in [Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*, 2007 †](https://heathbrothers.com/books/made-to-stick/). MODERATE to STRONG for the bias itself.

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**J1. Technical-term inventory.** List every term that is not in a basic vocabulary of Italian. A suggested resource is De Mauro's *Nuovo vocabolario di base della lingua italiana* (2016, about 7,500 words; † Internazionale/Treccani). Check for each term: (a) Is it necessary, or does an everyday word exist ("corteccia prefrontale" may be needed; "processi omeostatici" can usually become "il modo in cui il corpo si riequilibra")? (b) If it is necessary, is it introduced with a *concrete* definition (K1) at first use and re-glossed briefly when reused after a long gap?

**J2. Jargon budget.** Since even defined jargon can cost fluency (Shulman 2020), keep the number of retained technical terms low. HEURISTIC: ≤ 1 new technical term per paragraph (consistent with L1), and a chapter-level glossary of no more than about 5–8 terms the reader really needs to keep.

**J3. Definition quality.** A good definition is concrete, short, given before or at the term's first use, and uses words from the basic vocabulary. Flag circular definitions, definitions made of other jargon, and definitions placed long after the term's first use.
- FAIL: "L'interocezione, cioè la percezione degli stati fisiologici interni mediata da afferenze viscerali, …"
- PASS: "L'interocezione — la capacità di sentire che cosa succede dentro il corpo: il cuore che accelera, lo stomaco che si chiude — …"

**J4. Business jargon cuts both ways.** Managerial jargon and anglicisms ("engagement", "KPI", "mindset", "stakeholder") are in-group language for SME managers. Use them when the readers actually use them, but do not stack them. Mixing them with neuroscience jargon adds processing load.

**J5. Curse-of-knowledge check.** Ask the LLM to read each explanation "as a manager with no psychology background" and list the steps of reasoning the text skips. Flag implicit premises ("come sappiamo, il sistema limbico…") and phrases like "ovviamente", "come noto" or "semplicemente" placed before a non-obvious point.

### Gaps
- No Italian-language studies on jargon and fluency were found.
- The Bullock 2019 study's handling of definitions was not confirmed in detail; the "even when defined" finding is from Shulman et al. 2020.
- The availability and licence of the De Mauro NVdB list for automated use was not verified.

## 10. Retention aids within nonfiction (retrieval practice, summaries, advance organizers, spacing)

### Takeaway
The most robust learning techniques are retrieval practice (testing yourself) and spacing (revisiting a concept after a delay). Both have meta-analytic support and work with prose. Advance organizers have a small but positive average effect. Summaries *written by the learner* have low utility for most readers, but author-provided summaries and headings act as signals that help memory for structure. A nonfiction book can build retrieval and spacing into its structure: end-of-chapter questions, callbacks to earlier concepts in new chapters and spaced re-encounters with the core concepts.

### Cited Findings
- **Testing effect with prose.** Students who read prose passages and were tested on them recalled more after 1 week than students who restudied them, though restudy was better at a 5-minute test (approximately 56% vs 42% recall at one week in Experiment 1 †) — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Psychological Science 17(3):249–255 †](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x). STRONG.
- **Meta-analyses of practice testing.** Practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions, with effects moderated by test features and feedback — [Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, Review of Educational Research 87(3):659–701, doi 10.3102/0034654316689306 [V]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); [WSU PDF [V]](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/). Note: secondary sources disagree on the number of studies included (118 vs 217). Overall effect in another meta-analysis: g ≈ 0.50 — [Rowland 2014, Psychological Bulletin 140(6):1432 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559). STRONG.
- **Spacing.** A meta-analysis of 317 experiments (in 184 articles) showed spacing benefits on retention. The optimal gap scales with the retention interval — [Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(3):354 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). STRONG.
- **Utility ratings.** Practice testing and distributed practice are rated high utility; summarization, highlighting and rereading low — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 †](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266).
- **Advance organizers.** Introductory material at a higher level of abstraction that bridges what the learner knows and the new material — [Ausubel 1960, Journal of Educational Psychology 51(5):267–272 †](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046669). A meta-analysis of 135 studies found a small positive average effect on learning and retention (approximately 0.2 SD †) — [Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980, American Educational Research Journal 17(2):211–218 †](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211). MODERATE (consistent but small).
- **Pre-questions.** Asking questions before reading boosts learning of the pre-questioned content, and in some studies of related content too — [Pan & Carpenter 2023, Educational Psychology Review 35, article 97 †](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5). MODERATE.
- **Signaling via headings and overviews** (see section 3) — [Lorch 1989 †](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320135).

### Inferences (checks an LLM can apply)
**R1. Chapter opener (advance organizer and pre-question).** Each chapter opens, within the story or right after it, with an organizer linking to something the manager already knows ("Sapete già che sotto pressione si decide peggio; questo capitolo spiega perché") and, optionally, one question the chapter will answer. Flag chapters that start explaining without any frame.

**R2. Retrieval prompts.** Each chapter ends with 2–4 *retrieval* questions that require recall or application ("Ripensa all'ultima trattativa difficile: che cosa ha fatto la tua amigdala nei primi secondi?"), not merely reflection or rereading. HEURISTIC count. Flag summaries that only restate content without asking the reader to retrieve it.

**R3. Spaced callbacks.** Maintain a concept register: the chapter where each core concept is introduced, plus the chapters where it is reused. HEURISTIC: each core concept should reappear, recalled briefly and applied to a new situation, in at least 2 later chapters, with increasing gaps (for example the next chapter, then 2–3 chapters later). Flag core concepts that never reappear after their introduction, and also those re-explained in full every time (redundancy for readers who already know them).

**R4. Author summaries.** End-of-chapter summaries should be short (3–5 key points), stated as claims rather than topics, and in the same words as the key-point signals in the text (L3b). They help structure (signaling) even though learner-generated summarization is low utility.

**R5. Story as retrieval cue.** Use the character's name and scene as a retrieval cue when recalling a concept later ("ricordate la telefonata di Marco con il fornitore?"). This combines a concrete cue (section 2) with spacing.

### Gaps
- No studies were found specifically on embedded retrieval questions in trade nonfiction books read voluntarily (as opposed to students who are tested). Their transfer to that setting is an inference.
- The exact overall effect size of Adesope et al. 2017 and the study-count discrepancy were not resolved. Luiten 1980 and Roediger & Karpicke numbers are †.
